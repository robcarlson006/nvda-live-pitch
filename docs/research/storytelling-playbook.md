# Storytelling playbook for the NVIDIA pitch

Research notes, 23 Nov 2026. Purpose: decide HOW to tell the story before rewriting the script. Audience: a sceptical, ESG-focused board.

**Method note:** Obscura (headless browser) is not installed here, so this was done with web search plus page fetches. Sources are marked by strength. Book content came from summaries, not the books themselves, so check wording before quoting anyone on a slide.

## 1. What the academic research says

Main source read in full: Green & Appel (2024), "Narrative transportation: how stories shape how we see ourselves and the world", *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* (preprint PDF, mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de).

| Finding | Why it matters for us |
|---|---|
| **Transportation** = attention, emotion and imagery all focused on the events of the story (Green & Brock 2000, 2002). | The goal of the opening is to get the board *inside* the story, not to inform them. |
| Transported people **counterargue less**, and stories that show consequences through events trigger **less reactance** than didactic messages (section 3.4). | This is the single best reason to tell a story to a sceptical board. A claim they would resist as an argument gets through as an event. |
| Even clearly persuasive stories still work (3.4; Ratcliff & Sun 2020 meta-analysis on resistance). | We do not need to hide that we are pitching. |
| A story needs a **character with a goal, a conflict, and a turning point** (narrativity, around p.18). More of those elements means more narrativity. | Every act needs a character (Jensen, the Stanford team, Alex Krizhevsky), a problem, and a turn. |
| **Identification**: audiences take on the character's perspective and goals. First-person and similarity to the audience raise it (1.5.1). | Tell it through people the board can identify with: underdogs, a student with two gaming cards. |
| **Transitional characters** who begin sceptical and then convert were *more* effective than ones who were positive from the start (3.3, vaccine study, Huang & Green). | Let the board hear their own doubt first. "Wall Street hated it" is exactly this beat. The sceptic's view is voiced, then answered. |
| **Imagery**: vivid pictures aid persuasion, and narrative-relevant images increase imagery fluency and later persuasion (3.2). | Supports the live-built visuals. Each image should show the story moment, not decorate it. |
| Transportation is **disrupted** when content contradicts the audience's own experience, and by jumbled event order (1.2). | Don't claim something they can check and know is false. Keep the chronology clean. |

Supporting, weaker evidence from search (popular write-ups, not read at source):
- Paul Zak: a story with a dramatic arc raised cortisol and oxytocin where a flat version did not; the donation effect came from intranasal oxytocin (+56% giving). Mostly Zak's own essays. Correlational for the narrative part. The "261% more likely to give" figure could not be verified, so **do not use it**. "Neural coupling" was not tied to Zak.
- Mixed evidence: several studies fail to find transportation as the mediator, so don't promise a mechanism on stage.

**Takeaway:** a story lowers resistance, which is the exact problem with an ESG board that expects to be sold to.

## 2. What stand-up comedians do

Sources: Chris Head on telling a story in stand-up (Chortle, 2022) and The Pudding's structural analysis of Ali Wong's *Baby Cobra* (2018). Both fetched. Other results (Toastmasters, MasterClass, Scribd guides) were skimmed only.

- **SREP: Set-up, Reveal, Escalation, Payoff.** Give facts only and hold back the point. One central idea, then make it bigger each beat. End on a final twist that reflects back on the reveal.
- **Misdirection through attitude.** Start with one stance, then turn. The turn lands harder because the audience already bought the first.
- **Don't announce the punchline.** No "you won't believe this". Let them find it.
- **Key word last.** End the line on the word that carries the surprise.
- **The callback is the closer.** The biggest laugh in *Baby Cobra* ties back to three earlier topics. Accumulated setups pay off at once. Closing on a callback to the opening gives a sense of closure.
- **Continuity across digressions.** Each topic flows into the next, so the audience keeps building a picture of the performer's worldview.
- **Rule of three:** two items set a pattern, the third breaks it. Guides warn it turns mechanical if used as a formula. Use it for emphasis, not everywhere.
- **Test the ending live.** Head found his payoff only by trying it on an audience. We should rehearse aloud and rewrite the ending from what lands.
- Not covered by what I read: pacing and laugh-density targets. Don't quote numbers on these.

## 3. Robert Greene, *The 48 Laws of Power*

Source: Shortform's summary page (fetched, all 48 laws listed, short paraphrases only). Not the book. Treat as orientation.

Useful for presenting:
- **Law 9, win through actions, never through argument:** demonstrate rather than argue. For us this means the live-built slide *is* the proof. Don't tell them AI is capable, let it run.
- **Law 37, create compelling spectacles:** visuals and symbols beat words. The stage build is a spectacle.
- **Law 29, plan all the way to the end:** know the last line first and write backwards. We already have it.
- **Law 6, court attention:** being ignored is the worst outcome. The "built live" reveal earns the room's attention.
- **Law 25, re-create yourself / Law 34, be royal:** project confidence, own the frame. Students pitching professionals should carry themselves as the people who built something.
- **Law 32, play to people's fantasies:** offer a picture of a future, against a gloomy present.

Use with care or avoid:
- **Laws 21 (seem dumber), 11 (keep people dependent), 3 (conceal your intentions), 43 (play on emotions and self-interest).** These rely on concealment. A sceptical, ESG-minded board that catches it will punish it. Our closing reveal is the opposite: everything is shown, and the reveal is *disclosure*, not trickery.

## 4. Dale Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People*

Source: a community notes gist of the 20 principles in Part 3 (order as given there) plus a Shortform summary page. Numbering varies by edition. The chapter text itself could not be read (the chapter page returned 401), so what follows on "dramatize" is the principle title only.

Part 3 principles that apply:
- **Begin in a friendly way. Get the other person saying "yes, yes".** Open on something the board already agrees with.
- **Never say "you are wrong". Show respect for their opinion.** Name their scepticism before we answer it.
- **Let the other person feel the idea is theirs.** Lead them to the conclusion with questions or a set-up, and let them reach it. The Shortform page frames this as asking a series of questions that lead to your conclusion.
- **Be sympathetic with their ideas and desires. Appeal to the nobler motives.** For this board, nobler motives are real: progress, stewardship, efficiency.
- **Dramatize your ideas. Throw down a challenge.** Make benefits vivid and visible. One summary describes dramatizing as making benefits more obvious, interesting and vivid.
- **Talk about your own mistakes first / volunteer the downsides.** The Shortform checklist says to volunteer the downsides of your position. We should say the risks before they do.
- From Part 1 (via Shortform): people want to feel important, and people care about what *they* want. Frame NVIDIA around what the board wants, not what we like.

## 4A. Round 2 (obscura): Chappelle, Schulz, books, speeches

Fetched with Obscura 0.2.7 (rendered pages, text dump). Where a claim rests on one blog or a summary, it says so.

### Chappelle: the early plant, the late payoff

- **Your observation holds, with one verified example.** Charles Ekokotu's breakdown of the last 15 minutes of *The Closer* (a blog, one reader's analysis) shows the mechanism. Chappelle tells the Daphne story using "she" throughout, builds her up, then ends with the line to her daughter, "I knew your father". The pronoun set the audience's assumption early and the last line reverses it. That is misdirection planted in the first minute of the story and paid off in the final line.
- **Chain-links, not a list.** A review of *The Unstoppable* describes him moving story to name, name to law, law to headline, headline to anecdote, then looping back to history. The same review says he announces "two closers" and the second act turns into a tight, darker monologue. The ending is a warning phrase set up by what came before.
- **Reviewers also describe callback endings.** A review of *The Closer* calls its ending "obviously a callback", and one on *The Age of Spin* says the special is organised around four meetings with O.J. with his home life as the backbone. These are single reviews, not analysis by comedy scholars.
- **What I could not confirm:** that Chappelle's *openers* are deliberately seeded for the finale across his specials. The Ringer review only confirms the Kendrick Lamar epigraph at the start of *Sticks and Stones*, and it is mostly a negative review. Comedipedia tags nearly every Chappelle bit it analysed (98 bits, 1998–2004) as "incongruity-resolution": a setup that points one way and a trigger line that forces a reinterpretation. That is per-joke structure, not the whole-set callback.
- **Craft rules that make a callback work** (from a long comedy-craft guide, arjankc.com.np, secondary): the original must be specific and memorable, there must be a real gap before it returns (too soon kills the surprise), and the closer should be the strongest material, often calling back to earlier bits for closure. Openers must be robust enough to win over a cold, sceptical room.
- **Tags:** one extra punchline after a finished joke, riding the same momentum, with no new setup needed. Professional target quoted in that guide: about 5 laughs per minute (a figure from a guide, not measured by me).

### Andrew Schulz

- **Primary source (his own words, via a Flagrant podcast summary and transcript):** you need a beginning, middle and end. Plant something early so that people know the story is over. He likes a "hot take baked into a story" and says the best bits are where the two merge. His line on endings: plant a sapling early and harvest it at the end, and it can't be too big.
- **Esquire interview:** he says he had never been a storyteller and read books about storytelling to learn. His special *Life*, about his and his wife's struggle to conceive, was his first single straight-through narrative. He says the audience didn't feel he was "trying to trick them", because he was sharing something that mattered and it also happened to be funny. Esquire's framing: before this, his routines were a series of loosely related jokes.
- **Lesson for us:** a stake that is real and personal makes the audience stop watching for the trick. Schulz's trade-off was to use his own vulnerable material. For three students pitching NVIDIA, the personal stake could be our own position as people who built this with AI.
- Not found: a structural breakdown of *Life*. The Hot Breath podcast page only has a summary.

### How the best books are written

Sources: Robert McKee's *Story* (via Derek Sivers' notes, a collected set of quotes) and the Shortform guide to fiction craft covering McKee, Stephen King, Lisa Cron and others. Both are summaries; Vonnegut's story-shape lecture could not be sourced (the page I tried was missing), so I'm not using it.

- **Character, want, obstacle, consequence.** McKee's questions: who, what do they want, why, how do they go about it, what stops them, what are the consequences.
- **Every scene must turn.** Check whether the value at stake is different at the end from the start. If not, cut it. A story is one big master event: the situation at the start versus the end should show an irreversible change.
- **Select a few moments but give us a lifetime.** Don't tell everything that happened. Truth is what we think about what happens, and "it actually happened" is a weak reason to include something.
- **Trivial told brilliantly beats profound told badly** (McKee). Craft is what holds the audience.
- **Three essentials** (Shortform's synthesis): a relatable protagonist, a goal blocked by obstacles, a theme. The audience feels the importance of events through the protagonist's emotions. Themes work best when they're values shared with the audience.
- **Unique voice:** a good story also tells the audience something about you. It humanises you.
- **Write, then read it back.** McKee's loop: create, then critique, rewrite. Combined with Chris Head's advice to test endings live, rehearsal is part of the writing.

### Obama's speeches

Sources: Roy Peter Clark's rhetorical analysis of the 2008 race speech (Poynter, read in full), a Wikipedia summary of his campaign rhetoric, and the 2004 DNC keynote text (Bill of Rights Institute edition).

- **Four strategies in the race speech** (Clark): allusion to shared patriotic texts, parallel constructions, a "two-ness" texture that holds both sides, and himself as a character in the story.
- **Open on what everyone shares.** The 2008 speech starts from the Constitution's preamble and returns to it as a refrain. The 2004 keynote opens with "my presence on this stage is pretty unlikely" and a family story, then says his story is part of the larger American story.
- **Concede the hard truth, then restore the hope.** The race speech names the original sin of slavery, then goes back to patriotic language to avoid a dark vision losing the listener. This is the transitional-character move from Green & Appel, in rhetoric form.
- **Parallelism and triads:** Clark counts five parallel phrases in one 43-word sentence ("more just, more equal, more free..."). Parallel structure makes ideas memorable and sounds better than it reads. The keynote uses it too: the "if there's a child on the south side of Chicago who can't read, that matters to me" run.
- **Hold two things at once** (balance, not either/or): "not a liberal America and a conservative America, but the United States of America."
- **The story at the end.** Clark notes the Ashley Baia story sits last on purpose: a young volunteer, then the older man who says "I am here because of Ashley". Put the human story where the argument lands.
- **Motifs.** The campaign summary says his recurring concepts (change, hope) became the campaign's themes, and "voicing" other people's stories gave the audience a role.
- **Process:** per one account, he largely wrote the 2004 keynote himself, and it was cut from about 25 minutes to 17 after two weeks of edits. The detail is secondary, from a search summary only.

### The shape of great talks (Duarte)

Sources: Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* via Tom Tunguz's summary and Maria Popova's article on her MLK analysis (both read).

- **Sparkline:** a great talk alternates between "what is" and "what could be", and ends on the future. Duarte maps MLK's "I Have a Dream" with rapid switching and repetition, and Steve Jobs' iPhone keynote the same way.
- **Audience as hero, speaker as mentor.** Paint the ordinary world, call the audience to adventure, handle their objections early, then establish yourself as the guide.
- **Situation, complication, question, answer.** Simplicity: "complexity kills presentations".
- **Metaphor:** about 20% of MLK's speech was metaphorical, and his bad-cheque metaphor three minutes in drew the first big applause (Duarte via Popova).

## 4B. What round 2 adds up to

1. **Plant, then harvest.** Chappelle and Schulz both do it. A small specific detail early, a payoff late. Schulz: keep the seed small.
2. **The ending recontextualises the opening.** It isn't just a repeated line. The audience hears the early moment differently once they know the end.
3. **Make the stake personal and real.** Schulz's special worked once the audience stopped suspecting a trick.
4. **Concede, then lift.** Obama names the hard truth first. A sceptical board will trust the hope more after they hear us say the risks.
5. **Alternate what is and what could be.** Duarte's sparkline gives each act a rhythm: current reality, then the world NVIDIA's bet made possible.
6. **Parallel phrasing and one refrain.** Pick one recurring phrase and bring it back, like Obama's preamble.
7. **Put the human story last.** The Ashley story pattern: the emotional proof comes after the argument.
8. **Every beat must change something.** McKee's value-shift test is a quick way to cut weak beats from an 11-minute draft that needs to be 3–4.

## 5. What all four say together

1. **Don't argue, show.** Stories (Green), demonstrations (Greene), dramatizing (Carnegie) and escalating a single idea (comedy) all agree.
2. **Voice the sceptic before the audience does.** Transitional characters (Green & Appel), "never say you are wrong" (Carnegie), misdirection (comedy). A conversion arc is more persuasive than an opening of pure enthusiasm.
3. **One character, one want, one obstacle, one turn per act.** Narrativity and SREP are the same shape.
4. **Plan the ending first, and pay it off with a callback.** Greene's Law 29, comedy's callback closer, and our closing line all point to this.
5. **Vivid and specific beats abstract.** Imagery research, Carnegie, and Law 37.
6. **Be honest where they can check.** Transportation breaks on contradiction, concealment tactics backfire with sceptics, and our own rule is no invented numbers.

## 6. Hooks for the NVIDIA script (suggestions, not edits)

These are ideas to decide on, not changes made to the script.

- **The underdog arc is already the strongest material.** The Denny's booth, the NV1 failure, and the activist fund doubting CUDA are the transitional character: Wall Street's view first, then the turn. The current draft has this. The ESG board can map "being doubted" onto their own stance.
- **Make the board's scepticism a character.** Open Act 2 by saying the board's doubt out loud ("a company spent a fortune on something gamers didn't want; you'd have sold too"), then reveal the turn (AlexNet).
- **Callback closing.** The live-built reveal can call back to the opening: three people in a booth at a Denny's, three students with a laptop. That echo is the comedy closer, and it stays inside the closing line (which still frames the AI industry as built on NVIDIA infrastructure, not that this app only runs on NVIDIA hardware).
- **Escalate one idea through the four layers.** Chips, software, connections, supply are the same point (a rival must beat all four) growing each time, ending on the biggest number.
- **Volunteer the downside before Q&A.** A short, honest risk beat (bubble, China, energy) said by us first fits Carnegie's "own mistakes first" and denies the board the gotcha. The ESG question about energy should be answered in the pitch, not only in Q&A.
- **Rule of three, used once or twice only.**
- **Rehearse aloud and rewrite from what lands** (rehearsal/ folder exists for this).

## 7. Open questions for Rob

- Is the board going to be told up front that the deck builds live, or is it a surprise reveal at the end? The research favours disclosure over concealment, but a reveal at the end is a legitimate story beat as long as nothing is hidden that matters to the decision.
- Who is the main character: Jensen, the company, or "the bet"?
- How many minutes does each speaker really have? The script file says Rob's slot is 3–4 minutes of a 10-minute pitch, and the current draft is about 11 minutes.
